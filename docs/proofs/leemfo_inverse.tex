\begin{abstract}
A low-energy electron microscope records pictures: brightness at each
pixel, at several focus settings.
Those pictures are not the sample.
The sample is encoded in a complex wave that left the surface; the
camera keeps only the brightness of that wave after the lenses have
blurred, filtered, and mixed its spatial frequencies.
This note explains, for a reader who has not met Fourier optics,
numerical inverse methods, or the Lean proof assistant, how to go
backwards: from a through-focal stack of intensities to the exit wave
$\psi_0=\sigma\,\ee^{\ii\phi}$, and from $\phi$ to topographic height
in reflection.
Yu, Lau and Altman~\cite{Yu2019} wrote the forward map (object to
image) and matched contrast; they did not invert.
The pipeline recommended here is two-stage.
Stage~1 is a regularized, frequency-by-frequency inversion of the
through-focal stack, using the same kernel as Yu~\emph{et al.}
restricted to the contrast-transfer slice.
It is the unique published style of 2D LEEM exit-wave reconstruction
(Duden, Thust, Kumpf and Tautz~\cite{Duden2014}) and it is the
fastest method that still fills the holes a single focus leaves in
the contrast-transfer function.
Stage~2 is an optional nonlinear refinement for strong-phase objects
(graphene ripples, atomic steps); it is skipped when the stage-1
prediction already matches the stack to within the noise
($\max\abs{\phi}\lesssim 0.3$).
A one-dimensional sinusoidal ripple, the example Yu~\emph{et al.}\
used to exhibit contrast-transfer failure, reduces stage~2 to fitting
a short ladder of Bessel amplitudes.
Lean~4 is used as a certificate that the discrete linear stage is
well-posed under that kernel---unique answer, exact error identity,
proved physical blind spots---not as a replacement for the
experiment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Who this is for}
\label{sec:who}

This note assumes you know what a microscope image is and why one
might record several of them at different focus.
It does not assume Fourier optics, numerical analysis, or any
experience with proof assistants.

Three ideas are introduced in order.
First the experiment: what a LEEM records, and what we want back
(\cref{sec:experiment,sec:want,sec:notenough}).
Then the optical model that sits between those two things, in one
page of Fourier optics (\cref{sec:fo,sec:inverting}).
Then Lean: what a machine-checked proof is, why it is useful here,
and exactly which claims about the numerical method have certificates
(\cref{sec:lean,sec:certify}).
Only after that do we compare reconstruction methods and write down
the recipe (\cref{sec:five,sec:verdict,sec:algo,sec:bias,sec:bessel}).

Equations are translations of the prose, not a substitute for it.
Formal theorem statements live in \cref{app:formal}; they are
optional.
The companion checklist for the machine-checked statements is
\texttt{docs/LINEAR\_INVERSE.md} in this repository.

\section{What the experiment records}
\label{sec:experiment}

Low-energy electron microscopy (LEEM) is a surface microscope.
A slow electron beam (typically a few electronvolts at the sample)
reflects from a crystalline surface and is then accelerated to
column energy, about $15\,\mathrm{kV}$, and imaged by electron
lenses~\cite{Bauer1994,Bauer2014,Tromp2000,Altman2010}.
Aberration-corrected instruments of the kind Yu, Lau and
Altman~\cite{Yu2019} model are described by Tromp and
coworkers~\cite{Tromp2010,Tromp2013}.

The detector is a camera.
At each pixel it records a real, nonnegative number: how many
electrons arrived, or equivalently the \emph{intensity}
$I(\mathbf{r})$.
It does not record the electron wave.
The wave has an amplitude and a phase; the camera keeps the squared
modulus after the lenses have acted, and throws the phase away.
That is the same limitation as an ordinary light camera, and it is
the reason reconstruction is a problem at all.

A \emph{through-focal series}, or focal stack, is the same field of
view recorded at several focus settings $\Dz_k$, $k=1,\dots,K$.
Typical numbers in this note are $K=11$--$21$ planes and
$N=512^2$ pixels.
Changing focus changes how the lenses delay off-axis rays relative
to the axis, so the same object produces a different interference
pattern in each plane.
Those differences are the extra information that makes inversion
possible.

Yu, Lau and Altman~\cite{Yu2019}, following Pang~\emph{et
al.}~\cite{Pang2009} and Schramm~\emph{et al.}~\cite{Schramm2012},
write a precise forward map from a trial object to such a stack and
compare the predicted contrast with experiment.
That is \emph{forward matching}.
This note is about the inverse: given the stack, recover the object.

\section{What we want back}
\label{sec:want}

Just above the surface the reflected electrons form a complex
\emph{exit wave}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:psi0}
\psi_0(\mathbf{r})=\sigma(\mathbf{r})\,\ee^{\ii\phi(\mathbf{r})}.
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma$ is a real amplitude and $\phi$ is a real phase in
\emph{radians}.
For a topographic height $A$ seen in reflection, the kinematic
phase is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:phiA}
\phi=\frac{4\pi A}{\lambda_0},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_0$ is the electron wavelength \emph{at the sample}.
At $E_0=10\,\mathrm{eV}$, $\lambda_0\simeq 0.39\,\mathrm{nm}$, so a
height of $0.1\,\mathrm{nm}$ already gives $\phi\sim 3$: not a small
perturbation.
Graphene ripples and atomic steps live in that regime~\cite{Yu2017}.

Two wavelengths appear in this subject, and mixing them is the most
common modelling error.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.25em]
\item $\lambda_0$ is the wavelength at the sample (a few
electronvolts).
It sets the object phase~\eqref{eq:phiA}.
\item $\lambda$ is the wavelength \emph{after acceleration} to
column energy $E$ (about $15\,\mathrm{kV}$).
It is a few picometres.
It sets the lens optics: defocus, spherical aberration, and the
meaning of a spatial frequency
$\mathbf{q}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}/\lambda$~\cite{Tromp2012,Schramm2012}.
\end{itemize}
The reconstruction kernel below uses $\lambda$.
The conversion of reconstructed phase to height uses $\lambda_0$.

The Fourier transform of $\psi_0$ is written $\boldsymbol{\Psi}$.
A spatial frequency $\mathbf{q}$ is a sinusoidal ripple across the
field of view: large $\abs{\mathbf{q}}$ is fine detail.

\section{Why one picture is not enough}
\label{sec:notenough}

Four independent losses of information sit between $\psi_0$ and a
single image $I(\mathbf{r},\Dz)$.

\paragraph{The camera drops phase.}
Intensity is $\abs{\text{wave in the image plane}}^2$.
Any global factor $\ee^{\ii\theta}$ on $\psi_0$ cancels and never
appears.
The reconstruction can at best recover $\psi_0$ up to one overall
phase, which we fix by a convention (the ``vacuum gauge'' of
\cref{sec:algo}).

\paragraph{The lenses filter.}
Each spatial frequency is multiplied by a complex factor that
depends on defocus and on residual aberrations.
That factor---introduced properly in \cref{sec:fo}---can be zero at
some $\mathbf{q}$.
Dividing the data by a zero is meaningless: those frequencies are
absent from that image.

\paragraph{The contrast aperture cuts.}
A physical aperture in the column passes only
$\abs{\mathbf{q}}\le\qap$.
Finer detail is discarded before the camera.
No later algorithm can invent it.
Those modes reconstruct as zero, and that is a prediction, not a
bug.

\paragraph{One complex measurement of two unknowns.}
A weak perturbation of a strong reflected beam produces, at each
frequency $\mathbf{q}$, a pair of unknown complex amplitudes
$\bigl(X(\mathbf{q}),X(-\mathbf{q})\bigr)$.
One image supplies one complex equation for that pair, so a whole
line of objects give the same picture.
A second, independent focus setting supplies a second equation.

The practical conclusion: record a stack with $K\ge 2$ defoci whose
filter zeros do not sit on top of each other, and do not ask the
reconstruction for detail beyond $\qap$ or for an absolute global
phase.

\section{Fourier optics in one page}
\label{sec:fo}

Fourier optics is the statement that an image is interference.
Goodman's textbook~\cite{Goodman2005} is the standard reference in
light optics; the LEEM versions are Pang~\emph{et
al.}~\cite{Pang2009}, Schramm~\emph{et al.}~\cite{Schramm2012},
Yu~\emph{et al.}~\cite{Yu2017,Yu2019}, and the $\chi$-in-waves
convention of Tromp and Schramm~\cite{Tromp2012}.

Decompose the exit wave into spatial frequencies $\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{q})$.
In the image plane, every pair of frequencies $(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}')$
beats against each other and produces a fringe at
$\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}'$.
The strength of that fringe is a complex weight
$\RFO(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}',\Dz)$, the \emph{Fourier-optics kernel}.
The recorded intensity is the sum of all such fringes:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:I}
I(\mathbf{r},\Dz)
=\iint
\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{q})\,\boldsymbol{\Psi}^*(\mathbf{q}')
\,\RFO(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}',\Dz)
\,\ee^{2\pi\ii(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}')\cdot\mathbf{r}}
\,\dd^2q\,\dd^2q'.
\end{equation}
The kernel is supported on the aperture
$\abs{\mathbf{q}},\abs{\mathbf{q}'}\le\qap$.
In the notation of Yu~\emph{et al.}~\cite{Yu2019} it factors as
$\RFO=R_0 E_S E_{C,\mathrm{tot}}$: a perfectly coherent piece $R_0$
times a spatial (source-size) envelope $E_S$ times a chromatic
(energy-spread) envelope $E_{C,\mathrm{tot}}$.
Those envelopes are closed-form Gaussians; they are proved in the
companion note~\cite{Lau2026proofs} and encoded in this repository.

Two pictures of the same optics are in circulation.

\paragraph{Contrast-transfer function (CTF).}
Pretend each frequency travels alone: multiply $\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{q})$
by a filter $H(\mathbf{q},\Dz)$, inverse-transform, and take the
modulus squared.
This is accurate when the illumination is perfectly coherent, and
it remains a good linearization when the object is a \emph{weak}
perturbation of a flat mirror (small $\phi$).
Schramm~\emph{et al.}~\cite{Schramm2012} develop that description
for aberration-corrected LEEM and PEEM.

\paragraph{Fourier optics (FO).}
Keep the pairs.
Partial coherence---a finite source and a finite energy
spread---mixes $\mathbf{q}$ with $\mathbf{q}'$, so the weight
$\RFO(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}')$ does not factor as
$H(\mathbf{q})\overline{H(\mathbf{q}')}$.
Yu~\emph{et al.}~\cite{Yu2017} showed that this mixing is why a CTF
calculation misses the contrast of rippled graphene.
The FO kernel agrees with the CTF kernel on the slice
$\mathbf{q}'=\mathbf{0}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:slice}
\RFO(\mathbf{q},0,\Dz)=\RCTF(\mathbf{q},\Dz).
\end{equation}
The other axis $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$ is the conjugate branch
$\RFO(0,-\mathbf{q})$, not the CTF.
Stage~1 below inverts the shared $\mathbf{q}'=\mathbf{0}$ slice.
Stage~2 uses the full pair kernel, which is what Yu~\emph{et al.}
actually compared with experiment.

\paragraph{Lens phase, in waves.}
The lenses delay a ray by an optical path $\chi$, measured in
\emph{waves} (path divided by $\lambda$), not in radians.
The transfer factor is $W=\ee^{2\pi\ii\chi}$~\cite{Tromp2012}.
Defocus enters as $+\tfrac12\Dz\lambda q^2$ in $\chi$.
That sign is a convention of this library: positive $\Dz$ adds path
for off-axis rays.
Some published simulators use the opposite sign; mixing the two
swaps overfocus and underfocus without changing any plot label.
Do not convert $\chi$ to radians and then insert another $2\pi$.

\section{What ``inverting'' means}
\label{sec:inverting}

Given $I(\mathbf{r},\Dz_1),\dots,I(\mathbf{r},\Dz_K)$, find an exit
wave $\psi_0$ whose predicted stack, through~\eqref{eq:I}, matches
the measurements as well as the noise allows.

That is not what Yu, Locatelli and Altman~\cite{Yu2017} or Yu, Lau
and Altman~\cite{Yu2019} did.
They chose a trial object (a step, a sinusoid), ran~\eqref{eq:I}
forwards, and overlaid predicted versus measured contrast against
defocus.
The only demonstrated 2D experimental LEEM exit-wave reconstruction
is Duden, Thust, Kumpf and Tautz~\cite{Duden2014}: a linear
through-focal restoration of a molecular monolayer, in the
tradition of Schiske's focus-series reconstruction in electron
optics~\cite{Schiske1968,Schiske1973}.
Duden~\emph{et al.}\ assume a strong specular beam and a linear
(CTF-style) model.

This note is the inverse of the Yu~2019 forward model.
Stage~1 is Duden's algorithm with the FO/CTF envelopes of this
library in place of a coherent probe.
Stage~2 is the refinement required when the object is too strong
for that linearization---the regime that made FO necessary in the
first place.

\section{What Lean is, and why it is here}
\label{sec:lean}

A \emph{proof assistant} is a computer program that checks a
mathematical argument the way a compiler checks types: every
inference must follow from stated definitions and previously
accepted lemmas, or the file is rejected.
Lean~4 is such a system~\cite{deMoura2021}.
Its companion library Mathlib is a large, continuously checked
corpus of algebra, analysis, and special
functions~\cite{mathlib2020}.
This repository uses Lean~4 with Mathlib to encode the Yu~2019
kernel and the discrete reconstruction below.
Surveys of why machine-checked proofs matter in scientific
computing are given by Harrison~\cite{Harrison2008} and by Avigad
and Harrison~\cite{AvigadHarrison2014}.

That is a different activity from writing a numerical script.
A script evaluates numbers: it can plot a through-focal series,
invert a matrix, or report a residual.
It does not, by itself, certify that the kernel on the screen is
the kernel in the paper, that the regularized inverse is the unique
minimizer of the energy we wrote down, or that an error bound is an
identity rather than a lucky observation on one dataset.

The LEEM inverse is exactly the setting in which that distinction
bites.
Papers treat uniqueness, aperture support, and ``one image is
enough'' as routine, and those are the places the conventions of
\cref{sec:want,sec:fo} go wrong: $\lambda$ versus $\lambda_0$,
$\chi$ in waves, CTF versus FO, and the sign of defocus.
A Python reconstruction can run, produce a plausible-looking
height map, and still have implemented the wrong kernel.
Lean does not replace the experiment or the discrete Fourier
transform.
It certifies the \emph{algebra of the method}, pinned to the same
$\RFO$ already proved in this repository~\cite{Lau2026proofs}.

What, then, does it mean to say that the ``numerical proof is
good''?
It does not mean that a reconstruction looks plausible.
It means three things a script can illustrate but cannot settle.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.35em]
\item The regularized estimator is \emph{unique}: for every
frequency bin and every positive regularization there is exactly
one best object, given by a closed formula.
\item The error of that estimator is an \emph{identity}: the
difference between the estimate and the true bin is exactly a
projected leftover minus a bias term, over a positive denominator.
\item The method has \emph{physical blind spots} that no amount of
regularization removes---modes outside the aperture, a global
phase, a single image of a general complex object, and the
interior zeros of a single-defocus contrast-transfer function.
\end{enumerate}
Those are the claims Lean locks down.
A later implementation can be checked against those certificates,
not only against a plot.
The numerical method remains the method; Lean is the certificate
that we are running the method we think we are running.

\section{What Lean certifies, so the numerical method is trustworthy}
\label{sec:certify}

The discrete linear stage is recommended because the following
statements have been checked, not because a reconstruction on one
stack looked clean.
They are listed in the order a microscopist meets them.
Full statements are in \cref{app:formal}.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.55em]
\item \textbf{Same forward model.}
The reconstruction uses the bilinear kernel of Yu~\emph{et al.}
and the identity~\eqref{eq:slice}: $\RFO$ and $\RCTF$ coincide on
the slice $\mathbf{q}'=\mathbf{0}$.
Stage~1 inverts that slice, not a generic blur and not a
substitute propagator.

\item \textbf{A well-posed discrete problem.}
After a discrete Fourier transform, each spatial frequency
decouples.
For a positive regularization parameter the energy that measures
``predicted minus measured, plus a small penalty on the object''
has a unique global minimizer, given in closed form.
The computer is not choosing among several equally good objects.

\item \textbf{An exact error formula.}
If the data equal the linear model plus a leftover $n$ (camera
noise, discretization, and the part of FO that the linearization
dropped, lumped together), the estimation error is exactly
(projected leftover minus a regularization bias) over a positive
denominator.
This is algebra, not a simulation, and it is not a statistical
noise theorem: $n$ need not be random or Gaussian.

\item \textbf{A usable bound.}
The size of that error is no worse than a simple triangle
inequality, and the bound is attained on an explicit example.
That is the certificate that regularization plus stacked defoci
controls the mistake.

\item \textbf{Physical limits, not software bugs.}
Frequencies outside the contrast aperture have a vanishing slice
at every defocus, so those bins reconstruct as zero.
Intensity is unchanged if the exit wave is multiplied by a global
phase.
One image never identifies a general complex pair
$\bigl(X(\mathbf{q}),X(-\mathbf{q})\bigr)$.
The weak-phase contrast-transfer factor $\sin(2\pi\chi_S)$ vanishes
on rings inside a large enough aperture of a \emph{pure-defocus}
column ($C_3=C_5=0$, $\Dz\neq 0$); those holes are why one stacks
at least two independent defoci.
(That is not the same as a column with spherical aberration still
present.)
A failed reconstruction at those places is predicted.

\item \textbf{When the linear method is allowed.}
The FO image of a small change around a flat mirror is exactly
``vacuum intensity, plus a term linear in the change, plus a
quadratic remainder''.
Stage~1 inverts the linear term.
If the remainder is large (strong phase), the linear inverse is
biased---that is the license for stage~2.
If the remainder sits at the noise floor, stage~2 cannot help.

\item \textbf{A cost model, not a benchmark.}
Counting a modelled discrete Fourier transform as
$N\log_2 N$ operations, the linear stage is the cheap method that
still uses the whole stack and still fills contrast-transfer
zeros.
Already on a $128$-pixel grid it is strictly cheaper than summing
every frequency pair.
Lean does \emph{not} prove that a fast Fourier transform exists,
or that a laptop will take twenty milliseconds.

\item \textbf{Explicitly not proved.}
Alignment of the experimental stack; the map from lens current to
$\Dz$; equality of the array transform with the continuum Fourier
transform of the paper; Gaussian or Poisson statistics and
Cram\'er--Rao bounds; convergence of the nonlinear refinement;
and any claim to be fastest among all conceivable algorithms.
\end{enumerate}

The certificates live in \texttt{LeemFO/Tikhonov.lean} and
\texttt{LeemFO/LinearInverse.lean}; the sinusoid mode count and
Bessel expansion live in \texttt{LeemFO/Inverse.lean} and
\texttt{LeemFO/PhaseObject.lean}.
\Cref{app:formal} names the individual theorems.

\section{Five ways one might invert, in plain language}
\label{sec:five}

Write $N$ for the number of image pixels and $K$ for the number of
focus settings.
A typical LEEM stack is $N=512^2$ and $K\sim 20$.

The words \emph{regularization} and \emph{Tikhonov} will appear
once, then we stop leaning on them.
Inverting a filter that can be nearly zero is unstable: a small
error in a dark frequency becomes a huge error in the object.
Tikhonov's method~\cite{Tikhonov1963}---the same idea as Wiener's
filter in signal processing~\cite{Wiener1949}---adds a small
positive floor $\alpha$ in the denominator so one never divides by
zero.
The price is a systematic bias of size $\alpha$, which
\cref{sec:certify} already accounts for.

\subsection*{1. Single-image Wiener / contrast-transfer deconvolution}
Take one picture, transform it to frequencies, divide by the
filter, and transform back.
This is the fastest method, and it is the wrong inverse.
The filter zeros punch holes in the spectrum, and a general
complex object is not identified by one plane
(\cref{sec:notenough}).

\subsection*{2. Transport of intensity}
Teague~\cite{Teague1983} and Paganin and
Nugent~\cite{Paganin1998} recover phase from two or three closely
spaced defoci by solving a Poisson equation, under the assumption
that intensity and phase obey a simple paraxial continuity
equation.
After $15\,\mathrm{kV}$ acceleration a LEEM has
$\lambda\sim 10\,\mathrm{pm}$, a hard aperture, spherical
aberration, and partial coherence.
Transport of intensity ignores all of that.
It produces a low-pass phase map, not an inverse of~\eqref{eq:I},
and it has not been the method used on experimental LEEM graphene
or steps.

\subsection*{3. Multi-defocus regularized inversion (Schiske)}
\label{sec:schiske}
Transform every plane to frequencies.
Each spatial frequency $\mathbf{q}$ can then be treated on its
own.
The $K$ complex numbers $\hat I_k(\mathbf{q})$ are $K$ noisy
readings, each multiplied by the known slice
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:linear}
h_k(\mathbf{q})=\RFO(\mathbf{q},0,\Dz_k).
\end{equation}
If one pretends the unknown is a single complex amplitude
$X(\mathbf{q})$, the regularized estimate is the weighted average
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:xhat}
\hat x
=
\frac{\sum_k \overline{h_k}\,y_k}{\alpha+\sum_k\abs{h_k}^2}.
\end{equation}
The honest linearization around a flat mirror couples
$X(\mathbf{q})$ with its partner $X(-\mathbf{q})$
(\cref{sec:notenough}); that is a $2\times 2$ regularized solve
with the same floor $\alpha$, unique for every $\alpha>0$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:xhat} is the one-unknown special case.
If one focus has $h_k=0$, the others can still determine the bin.
That is what ``filling contrast-transfer zeros by stacking
defoci'' means.
The cost is one transform per plane, a few arithmetic operations
per frequency, and one inverse transform: milliseconds to a few
tens of milliseconds on a laptop for $N=512^2$ and $K\sim 20$.
This is Duden's algorithm~\cite{Duden2014} with the Yu~2019
envelopes, and it is the unique minimizer Lean already certified
(\cref{sec:certify}).
It is also exactly one step of the nonlinear method below, started
from a flat mirror.

\subsection*{4. Gerchberg--Saxton / hybrid input--output}
Gerchberg and Saxton~\cite{GerchbergSaxton1972} and
Fienup~\cite{Fienup1982} iterate between planes, replacing the
computed modulus by the measured one and propagating with a
coherent lens model.
Under partial coherence the correct intensity constraint is the
quadratic set of~\eqref{eq:I}, not a coherent modulus.
Naive modulus replacement is therefore the coherent CTF model, and
it is biased when FO and CTF disagree off
axis~\cite{Yu2017}.
If the iteration is rewritten to project onto the FO intensity,
it becomes a slower, undamped version of method~5.

\subsection*{5. Nonlinear least squares on bilinear FO}
Guess an exit wave, predict the whole stack with~\eqref{eq:I}, and
adjust the guess until the prediction matches the measurements.
Each adjustment (a Gauss--Newton step) uses the pair kernel, not
only the slice~\eqref{eq:linear}, so it sees the mixing that the
linear method drops.
That is the inverse of the kernel Yu~\emph{et al.}\ actually used,
and it is the right estimator for strong-phase objects.
It is no longer a per-frequency weighted average about a generic
background, so it is slower: seconds rather than milliseconds at
typical aperture sizes, for a handful of iterations started from
method~3.
If the aberrations themselves are unknown, the same idea stacked
over extra defoci is the maximum-likelihood (MAL) reconstruction
of Coene, Thust, Op de Beeck and Van Dyck~\cite{Coene1996}.
MAL is needed only when the lens coefficients are part of the
unknown.

\section{Verdict}
\label{sec:verdict}

\begin{center}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{2.4cm}p{2.6cm}p{3.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
Method & Typical cost & Fills filter zeros & Faithful for large $\phi$ \\
\midrule
1. Single-image Wiener & one transform & no & no \\
2. Transport of intensity & one transform & n/a (wrong model) & no \\
\textbf{3. Multi-defocus regularized} & \textbf{$K{+}1$ transforms} & \textbf{yes} ($K\ge 2$) & linearized only \\
4. Gerchberg--Saxton / HIO & many transforms & if $K\ge 2$ & only if the projector is FO \\
5. Nonlinear least squares on FO & seconds (pair sums) & yes & \textbf{yes} \\
Jacobi--Anger fit (1D sinusoid) & tiny linear algebra & n/a (parametric) & \textbf{yes} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A modelled operation count for method~3, compared with a dense
pair-by-pair apply, is stated in \cref{app:formal}.

\paragraph{Fastest usable method for a general 2D experimental stack.}
Method~3: multi-defocus regularized inversion on the
contrast-transfer slice.
It is the cheapest algorithm that (i)~reads all $KN$ frequency
samples, (ii)~fills interior filter zeros by stacking defoci, and
(iii)~has a unique minimizer for every $\alpha>0$.

\paragraph{Best FO-faithful method.}
Method~3 as the starting guess, then a few damped Gauss--Newton
steps on method~5 (or MAL~\cite{Coene1996} if aberrations are
unknown).
Skip the nonlinear stage when the FO residual of method~3 is
already at the noise floor---equivalently
$\max\abs{\phi}\lesssim 0.3$ at typical LEEM signal-to-noise, i.e.\
topography $A\lesssim 0.01\,\mathrm{nm}$ at $E_0=10\,\mathrm{eV}$.

\paragraph{Fastest still-correct method for Yu's sinusoid.}
Fit the phase amplitude $\varphi$ on the
$M=2\lfloor \qap\Lambda\rfloor+1$ Bessel modes that the aperture
keeps (\cref{sec:bessel}).
That is method~5 restricted to a one-parameter family.

\section{Stage 1: the discrete recipe}
\label{sec:algo}

The following steps are the unique minimizer of
\cref{sec:certify}, written as an experimental procedure.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.4em]
\item Align the stack $I_k(\mathbf{r})$ by rigid shifts.
Alignment is preprocessing; it is not part of the certificate.
\item Subtract the spatial mean of each plane and take a discrete
Fourier transform.
The zero-frequency bin carries total flux
($\RFO(0,0)=1$) and is handled separately.
\item For every frequency $\mathbf{q}$ with $\abs{\mathbf{q}}\le\qap$
and every recorded defocus $k$, evaluate the closed-form slice
$h_k(\mathbf{q})=\RFO(\mathbf{q},0,\Dz_k)$ from the Yu~2019
envelopes, using column $\lambda$, $\chi$ in waves, and the
$+\tfrac12\Dz\lambda q^2$ sign.
\item At that bin, solve either the one-unknown
average~\eqref{eq:xhat} or the conjugate-pair $2\times 2$
regularized system (same $\alpha>0$; the $2\times 2$ system is
not~\eqref{eq:xhat}).
\item Set the reconstruction to zero for $\abs{\mathbf{q}}>\qap$.
\item Inverse-transform the assembled spectrum and read
$\sigma=\abs{\hat\psi}$ and $\phi=\arg(\hat\psi)$ in the vacuum
gauge: the zero-frequency component real and positive.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Units.}
The kernel $\RFO$ uses column wavelength $\lambda$ (after
acceleration).
Object phase uses $\lambda_0$: $\phi=4\pi A/\lambda_0$ for a
topographic height $A$ in reflection.

\paragraph{Defocus sign.}
This library uses $+\tfrac12\Dz\lambda q^2$ in the wave aberration.
Experimental lens current must be mapped onto that convention by
one fitted affine transform.
Mixing the minus-sign convention of some published simulators
reverses overfocus and underfocus.

\section{When stage 1 is biased}
\label{sec:bias}

Kinematic LEEM phase on reflection is~\eqref{eq:phiA}.
At $E_0=10\,\mathrm{eV}$, $A=0.1\,\mathrm{nm}$ already gives
$\phi\sim 3$.
The linear inverse never sees off-axis pairs
$(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}')$ where FO and CTF disagree.
That dropped piece is exactly the quadratic remainder Lean
certified in \cref{sec:certify}: the FO image of the increment
itself.
For a strong-phase sinusoid it is the whole
image~\cite{Yu2017}.

\paragraph{Skip test.}
Predict the stack from the stage-1 reconstruction using the full
FO kernel~\eqref{eq:I}.
If that residual is consistent with the noise, the quadratic
products are undetectable and stage~2 cannot help.
Do not skip merely because the slice~\eqref{eq:slice} looks
faithful: stage~1 never saw the off-axis pair envelopes
$\RFO(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}')$ with both frequencies nonzero.

\section{Stage 2, and the one-dimensional sinusoid}
\label{sec:bessel}

Stage~2 is the sentence ``adjust the guess until the predicted
images match the stack,'' with the prediction computed from the
full pair kernel~\eqref{eq:I}.
Started from stage~1, a few damped Gauss--Newton steps are the
usual implementation.
Convergence of that iteration is informal; the license to run it
is the quadratic remainder of \cref{sec:certify}.

If the object is known to be a single sinusoid of period
$\Lambda$ (Yu's example), the unknown collapses to an amplitude
$\varphi$ and a shift $x_0$.
The period is read from the fundamental of the intensity.
The aperture keeps only
\begin{equation}
M=2n_{\mathrm{ap}}+1,
\qquad
n_{\mathrm{ap}}=\bigl\lfloor \qap\Lambda\bigr\rfloor
\end{equation}
modes.
The FO image is then a double sum of $M^2$ terms, by the
Jacobi--Anger expansion
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:JA}
\ee^{\ii\varphi\sin(2\pi x/\Lambda)}
=\sum_{n\in\Z}J_n(\varphi)\,\ee^{2\pi\ii n x/\Lambda}.
\end{equation}
Fitting $\varphi$ with the analytic derivative
$J_n'=\tfrac12(J_{n-1}-J_{n+1})$ is a tiny least-squares problem.
Lean proves the mode count, the expansion, and the decay of the
dropped Bessel amplitudes.
This reduction does \emph{not} apply to a general 2D micrograph:
there is no Bessel ladder, and stage~2 stays a Gauss--Newton
iteration in the aperture basis.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:end}

For a 2D experimental LEEM focal series, recover the object in two
stages.
Stage~1 is a regularized, frequency-by-frequency inversion of the
through-focal stack on the contrast-transfer slice of the Yu~2019
kernel.
It fills the holes a single focus leaves, it has a unique answer
for every positive regularization, and it is the cheapest method
that still does those two things.
Stage~2 is a nonlinear refinement on the full Fourier-optics
kernel, used only when the stage-1 prediction disagrees with the
stack by more than the noise---the strong-phase regime of ripples
and steps.
For the one-dimensional sinusoid that exhibits contrast-transfer
failure, stage~2 collapses to a Bessel fit.

The linear stage is recommended because, under the Yu~2019 forward
model, it is well-posed: unique, equipped with an exact error
identity and a sharp bound, honest about aperture, gauge,
single-image, and filter-zero blindness, and first-order
consistent with bilinear Fourier optics.
Lean is the certificate of those statements, not a reason the
method happened to converge on one dataset.
Yu~\emph{et al.}\ forward-match; Duden~\emph{et al.}\ invert
linearly; the two-stage pipeline does both.

\appendix
\section{Formal claims}
\label{app:formal}

This appendix is optional.
The statements are finite-dimensional: a finite list of defoci and
a finite grid of frequencies.
Noise $n_k$ is a deterministic leftover (linearization remainder,
instrument noise, and discretization), not a random variable.
No existence theorem for the fast Fourier transform is claimed.

\begin{theorem}[Unique minimizer]
\label{thm:unique}
For $\alpha>0$ the energy
$J(x)=\sum_k\norm{h_k x-y_k}^2+\alpha\norm{x}^2$
has a unique global minimizer~\eqref{eq:xhat}.
Completing the square:
$J(x)=J(\hat x)+D\norm{x-\hat x}^2$ with $D=\alpha+\sum\abs{h_k}^2>0$.
\end{theorem}
Lean: \texttt{tikhonovJ\_min}, \texttt{tikhonov\_unique},
\texttt{tikhonovJ\_eq\_shift}.
The same identity gives uniqueness at $\alpha=0$ whenever
$\sum\abs{h_k}^2>0$.

\begin{theorem}[Bias--noise identity]
\label{thm:error}
If $y_k=h_k x^\star+n_k$ and $D\neq 0$, then
\begin{equation}
\hat x-x^\star
=
\frac{\sum_k\overline{h_k}\,n_k-\alpha x^\star}{D}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
Lean: \texttt{tikhonov\_error}.
Taking $\mathbb{E}[n]=0$ is informal (no probability space).

\begin{theorem}[Triangle bound, sharp]
\label{thm:bound}
If $\alpha\ge 0$ and $D>0$,
\begin{equation}
\abs{\hat x-x^\star}
\le
\frac{\sum_k\abs{h_k}\,\abs{n_k}+\alpha\,\abs{x^\star}}{D}.
\end{equation}
Equality holds for one measurement, $h=n=1$, $x^\star=-1$, $\alpha=1$.
\end{theorem}
Lean: \texttt{tikhonov\_error\_bound},
\texttt{tikhonov\_error\_bound\_sharp}.

\begin{theorem}[Quadratic remainder of bilinear FO]
\label{thm:quad}
With vacuum spectrum $1_{\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}}$ and increment $\delta$,
\begin{equation}
\hat I(\mathrm{vac}+\delta)
=
\hat I(\mathrm{vac})
+DI_{\mathrm{vac}}[\delta]
+\hat I(\delta),
\end{equation}
where $DI_{\mathrm{vac}}[\delta](\xi)=\RFO(\xi,0)\,\delta(\xi)+\RFO(0,-\xi)\,\overline{\delta(-\xi)}$
and $\abs{\hat I(\delta)(\xi)}\le\bigl(\sum\abs{\RFO}\bigr)\bigl(\sum\abs{\delta}\bigr)^2$.
\end{theorem}
Lean: \texttt{ihat\_add}, \texttt{ihatJac\_vacuum},
\texttt{ihat\_quadratic\_remainder}, \texttt{ihat\_bound}.
The dropped term is exactly bilinear: linearized inversion is biased by
$O(\norm{\delta}^2)$, which is the whole image for a strong-phase
sinusoid~\cite{Yu2017}.

\begin{theorem}[Interior CTF zeros of a pure-defocus column]
\label{thm:ctfzero}
$\sin(2\pi\chi_S)=0$ whenever $2\chi_S\in\Z$.
For $C_3=C_5=0$, $\Dz\neq 0$, and a large enough aperture, there exists
nonzero $q$ inside the disk with that vanishing
(\texttt{exists\_interior\_weakPhase\_zero}).
A single defocus is therefore blind at those rings; $K\ge 2$ with
incommensurate $\Dz$ typically fills them.
This is a pure-defocus hypothesis, not a column that still has
spherical aberration.
\end{theorem}

The $2\times 2$ Gram determinant of the vacuum Jacobian is positive for
$\alpha>0$ (\texttt{gramDet\_pos}), so the conjugate-pair normal
equations are invertible even though one measurement is not
(\cref{sec:notenough}).

\paragraph{Modelled cost.}
Set $\mathrm{dftCost}(N):=N\log_2 N$ and
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{reconstructCost}(K,N)
=(K+1)\,N\log_2 N+N(8K+4).
\end{equation}
For $K\ge 1$ and $N\ge 2$,
$\mathrm{reconstructCost}(K,N)\le 14\,K\,N\log_2 N$.
At $N=128$ this is strictly cheaper than a dense bilinear apply
$KN^2$, for every $K\ge 1$
(\texttt{reconstructCost\_le},
\texttt{reconstructCost\_lt\_dense\_128}).
The constant $14$ is attained in the model at $K=1$, $N=2$.
